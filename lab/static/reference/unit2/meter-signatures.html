Meter Review

There are two classifications for every meter:

  • Division structure – simple or compound
  • Metrical structure – duple or triple

Division Structure

Simple vs. Compound Meter

  • Simple meter: every beat divides into two division level parts
  • Compound meter: every beat divides into three division level parts

What does simple meter look like on the page?

Simple meter, with no meter signature:

♩ = 100
How can you tell this is a simple meter?
  • The beat level is the quarter note
  • Each beat divides into two division-level units (eighth notes)

We use beams in order to display the division structure

Notice that the beat level is beamed together, rather than flagging every eighth note separately:

♩ = 100

What does a compound meter look like on the page?

Compound meter, with no meter signature

♩ = 100
How can you tell this is in compound meter?
  • The beat level is the dotted quarter note
  • Each beat level divides into three division-level units (eighth notes)

Again, notice how each beat is beamed together, whenever possible, rather than flagging the division-level units separately

Metrical Structure

The other component necessary for determining a meter signature is the metrical structure

  • The metrical structure determines the hierarchical organization of beats within a measure.
  • It does not determine what is the duration of the beat level, or how that beat level breaks down into division-level units

What does a duple meter look like on the page?

First, determine the duration of the beat, then count how many beats are in a measure.

If there are 2 (or a multiple of 2) beats in each measure, the meter is duple.

♩ = 100
Analysis:
  • What is the beat level duration? Quarter note (we can tell by the beaming)
  • How many beats in every measure? 2 (we can tell by the bar lines)
  • Result: Duple meter

Determining the Meter Signature

After determining both the division and grouping structures, we can determine the meter signature.

Simple Meters

  • Top number: describes the number of beats in a bar
  • Bottom number: describes the duration of note that is at the beat level

Example 1: Meter Signature 2/4

  • Simple or compound meter? Simple
  • What is the duration of the beat level? Quarter note
  • What is the metrical organization of beats in the measure? 2 beats per measure
♩ = 100

Example 2: Meter Signature 4/4

  • Simple or compound? Simple
  • What is the duration of the beat level? Quarter note
  • What is the metrical organization of beats in the measure? 4 beats per measure
♩ = 100

Compound Meters

  • Top number: describes the number of division-level (NOT beat level) durations per measure
  • Bottom number: describes the duration of note that is at the division level (NOT beat level)

Example 3: Meter Signature 9/8

  • Simple or compound? Compound
  • What is the duration of the division level? Eighth note
  • How many division-level units per measure? 9
♩ = 100

Example 4: Meter Signature 6/8

  • Simple or compound? Compound
  • What is the duration of the division level? Eighth note
  • How many division-level units per measure? 6
♩ = 100

Helpful Hint

Note: A top number of 6 in a standard meter signature typically indicates a compound division structure.

Note: What we called the "grouping of pulses" when studying Timelines is essentially what defines the division level here. While this division-level notation stems from the history of Western music notation, the grouping of pulses into larger cycles is a fundamental organizing structure found in musical styles around the world.

Why Beaming Matters

Many rhythms can be beamed in different ways, but only some solutions keep the beat hierarchy obvious. If the notation hides the beat, performers struggle and misread.

Goal: always beam so the meter is visible at a glance. Good beaming reveals pulse; poor beaming hides it.

Show the Beat Level

Fundamental principle: beaming should reveal the metrical hierarchy.

Think of stacked “levels” when you notate rhythm in 4/4:

  • Half-note level: events on beats 1 or 3 sit one full level higher than the quarter-note beat.
  • Quarter-note level: beats 2 and 4 are the beat level itself.
  • Eighth-note level: the “and” of the beat sits one level lower than the beat.
  • Sixteenth-note level: the 2nd or 4th sixteenth inside a beat is another level lower.

Two-level test: if a note begins or ends on a level two degrees lower than a beat that it sustains through (e.g., a sixteenth tied across a quarter), split that note and tie it so the higher-level beat stays visible.

Don’t Contradict the Beat Level

Principle: notation should clarify the meter, not obscure it.

Use ties or regrouping any time a literal note value would hide the beat—a small tweak saves rehearsal time.

Quarter Notes in Compound Meters (6/8)

In compound meters, the beat is a dotted quarter (three eighths). Quarter notes can obscure that grouping if they are treated like independent beats.

Difference:

  • The “unusual” example obscures the dotted-quarter beat.
  • The “good” example ties through the beat, making the dotted-quarter pulse visible.

When can quarter notes work? When context already shows the compound beat or when ties clarify the dotted-quarter structure.

Example: Beaming Dotted Eighths in 4/4

Dotted eighth rhythms often hide the beat if the sixteenth component is grouped like a triplet instead of showing where the quarter-note beats fall.

The problem: the “BAD” bar ties the sixteenth to the dotted eighth within one beam, disguising where the quarter-note beats fall in 4/4. The “GOOD” version breaks and ties the note right on the beat so performers see every quarter instantly.

Rest Placement in Simple Meters (4/4)

Simple meters need visible beats, especially beat 3 in 4/4. Long rests that hide beat 3 disorient performers.

Fixes:

  • Use rests that cover whole beats (half rest = beats 1-2) before switching values.
  • Break longer rests so that beat 3 is still easy to spot.

Beat 3 is a strong metrical position (half-note level). Hide it and the ensemble loses its reference point.

The Golden Rule

It is not that certain note values are forbidden. The rule is: notation must make the metrical structure clear rather than obscuring it.

Test yourself: can a performer immediately see the beats? If yes, great. If not, re-beam or re-notate.

Summary: Beaming Principles

Compound Meters (6/8, 9/8, 12/8)

  • Beat = dotted quarter (three eighths).
  • Beam eighths in groups of three.
  • Beam sixteenths in groups of six.
  • Use ties if quarter notes threaten clarity.

Simple Meters (2/4, 3/4, 4/4)

  • Beat = quarter note (two eighths).
  • Beam eighths in pairs; beam sixteenths in fours.
  • Make beat 3 visible in 4/4.

Good beaming helps performers see beats, understand the meter instantly, and play rhythms accurately.

Irregular Meters

What Makes a Meter Irregular?

So far we have focused on meters where the beats are equal: simple (beats divide into 2) and compound (beats divide into 3). But some meters mix long and short beats inside the same measure.

  • Irregular (asymmetric) meters use beats of different lengths, usually in 3:2 or 2:1 ratios.
  • Common top numbers: 5, 7, 9, 10 (eighth- or quarter-based).
  • Examples: 5/8, 7/8, 5/4, 10/8 where the beaming shows how pulses are grouped.

5/8 = Unequal Beats

The measure contains five eighth notes. Since you cannot split 5 into equal halves, composers group the beats as 2+3 or 3+2.

Beat Structure 2 + 3: Beat 1 has two eighths (short) · Beat 2 has three eighths (long)

Beat Structure 3 + 2: Beat 1 is long (three eighths) while Beat 2 is short (two eighths).

7/8 Possibilities

Seven eighths can be partitioned several ways. Each metrical arrangement produces a distinct feel, signaled entirely by the beaming.

  • 2+2+3 – two short beats followed by a long one.
  • 3+2+2 – long-short-short.
  • 2+3+2 – short-long-short.

Composers choose the grouping to match the patterns in the music.

Link to Euclidean (Maximally Even) Rhythms

The onset patterns we studied with Euclidean rhythms often become the backbone of irregular meters.

E(3,7)

Pattern: x . x . x . .

Bulgarian ruchenitsa, Pink Floyd’s “Money” → 7/8 grouped as 2+2+3.

Pattern: x . x . x . x . .

Turkish aksak, Brubeck’s “Rondo à la Turk” → 9/8 (2+2+2+3).

E(5,8)

Pattern: x . x x . x x .

Cuban cinquillo → works naturally in 5/8.

Key idea: when a Euclidean onset pattern governs the entire measure, the music effectively lives in an irregular meter.

E(3,7) → 7/8

This pattern places three accents across seven pulses: short + short + long (2+2+3). The beaming spotlights those long/short beats.

Where You Hear Irregular Meters

  • Balkan/Eastern European folk: Bulgarian 7/8 & 9/8 dances, Greek 5/8 and 7/8.
  • 20th c. classical: Bartók’s string quartets, Stravinsky’s Rite of Spring.
  • Jazz/pop: Brubeck’s “Take Five” (5/4) and “Blue Rondo à la Turk” (9/8), Pink Floyd “Money” (7/4), Tool “Schism” (changing 6/8+7/8).

Reading Irregular Meter Signatures

  • Top number = total pulses per measure.
  • Bottom number = the note value of one pulse.
  • Look at the beaming: it reveals the long vs. short beats, just like our Euclidean diagrams.

Common groupings to memorize:

  • 5/8 → 2+3 or 3+2
  • 7/8 → 2+2+3, 3+2+2, 2+3+2
  • 9/8 → 2+2+2+3 (as in Blue Rondo) or other combinations

Summary: Simple meters divide beats into 2, compound meters divide into 3, and irregular meters mix long and short beats (grouping pulses in 2 and 3). Many of these patterns are exactly the maximally even rhythms we graphed last week.

Summary

  • Meter signatures are determined by division and metrical structures.
  • Beaming and bar lines clarify the meter in notation.
  • Simple meters divide beats into two; compound meters into three.
  • Duple and triple meters group beats per measure.
  • Irregular meters mix long and short beats (3 and 2 groupings), expressed in the beaming structure.
